Wiring a Single Axis
Step by Step

One rail, one motor, one driver — from bare parts to a jogging axis

Scope: A complete single-axis build: 24 V supply → FMC4030 controller → FMDD50D40NOM driver → rail (motor + limit switches).
Wiring order: 1 Power supply · 2 Controller · 3 Driver · 4 Rail. Each stage ends with a verification step before you move on.
Axis used: Controller axis 1 — terminals 5V / PU1 / DR1, limits LP1 / LN1. (In the SDK this is axis index 0.)
Everything stays de-energized until §8. You wire the whole axis with the AC plug out.
Companion document: the full Dual-X Gantry Assembly & Wiring Manual — this guide is the learning subset of it.

1How the four boxes connect

Before touching a screwdriver, get the shape of the system in your head. There are only four boxes, and each one has exactly one job:

BoxIts one jobWhat goes inWhat comes out
24 V supplyTurn wall AC into a 24 V DC bus100–220 VAC (L / N / earth)24 V+ and 24 V− that everything else drinks from
FMC4030 controllerThink. Decide where the axis should be24 V power, limit-switch signals, Ethernet commands from your PCA stream of 5 V pulse + direction signals
FMDD50D40NOM driverTurn tiny 5 V pulses into real motor current24 V power (muscle) + 5 V pulse/direction (instructions)Two coil currents, A and B, to the motor
The railTurn rotation into linear travel, and report its endsMotor coil currents; 24 V for the switchesMotion, plus limit/home signals back to the controller
┌──────────── everything drinks from this bus ────────────┐ │ │ AC 100–220 V ──► [ 24 VDC SUPPLY ] ══ 24V+ / 24V− ═══╦═══════════╦════════════╗ L N ⏚ ║ ║ ║ ▼ ▼ ▼ [ CONTROLLER ] [ DRIVER ] [ LIMIT SW ] FMC4030 FMDD50 FSK40 ×2 │ │ │ 5V / PU1 / DR1 └──────────►┤ │ (pulse + direction, 5 V) │ │ │ A+ A− B+ B− │ └──────► [ MOTOR ] on the RAIL LP1 / LN1 ◄─────── black signal wires ─────────┘ (the rail tells the controller where its ends are)
The one idea that makes all of this click Two completely separate things travel between the controller and the driver: instructions and muscle. The controller only ever sends instructions — a 5 V pulse train that carries almost no current (~15 mA). The muscle comes from the 24 V bus straight into the driver, and the driver decides how to push it through the motor coils. That is why the motor's power never flows through the controller, and why a mistake on the signal side makes the motor silent while a mistake on the power side makes it hot.

Why we wire in this order

Stage 1Power supplyBuild the 24 V bus and prove it is +24 V and correctly polarised, with nothing attached to damage.
Stage 2ControllerGive the brain power and earth, and a network cable. Nothing moves yet, so mistakes are cheap.
Stage 3DriverSet its DIP switches, feed it 24 V, then run the three signal wires from the controller.
Stage 4The railMotor coils into the driver, limit switches back to the controller. The moving parts go last.

Each stage is verifiable on its own. That is the whole point of the order: if something is wrong, you know it belongs to the stage you just finished, not to a haystack of forty wires.

Tools

Small flat and Phillips screwdrivers, hex keys, wire stripper/crimper, ferrules, a multimeter (you will use continuity and DC volts constantly), label tape or a marker, and a PC with an Ethernet port.

Label both ends of every wire as you make it. Two minutes with tape now saves an hour of tracing later. A single axis is 13 connections; write what each one is.

3Safety rules

⚠ The supply's input side carries 100–220 VAC. Do all wiring with the system fully de-energized and the AC plug physically removed — not just switched off. Mains termination should be done by someone qualified to do it. Never work on live terminals, and never leave AC terminals uncovered.

Do

  • Wire everything with power off, plug out.
  • Bond the controller's PE terminal and the machine frame to protective earth.
  • Check 24 V polarity with a meter before anything is connected to the bus.
  • Use ferrules on stranded wire entering screw terminals, and tug-test every wire.
  • Keep motor and 24 V power wiring physically away from signal and Ethernet runs.

Don't

  • Never plug or unplug the motor while the driver is powered — it can destroy the driver's output stage.
  • Don't exceed DC 50 V into the driver's V+/V−.
  • Don't split a motor coil pair across A and B (see §7).
  • Don't feed 24 V into the driver's PU/DR inputs — those are the 5 V side.
  • Don't make your first jog a long one. Millimetres first.

Stage 1The power supply

Goal: a 24 V DC bus you have measured and trust, with nothing else connected to it yet.

Everything downstream assumes this bus is correct. If the polarity is backwards here, you will find out by destroying a controller — so this stage is short, and it ends with a meter reading.

AC side (dangerous) DC side (safe to handle when AC is out) ─────────────────── ─────────────────────────────────────── L ──► L ┌─────────────────┐ V+ ──► 24V+ bus ══► controller, driver, switches N ──► N │ 24 VDC SUPPLY │ V− ──► 24V− bus ══► controller, driver, switches ⏚ ──► ⏚ └─────────────────┘ (some supplies label these +V / −V or DC OK)
Step 1.1 — Mount the supply and confirm its input voltage selector

Fix the supply in the cabinet where its terminals are reachable and its vents are not blocked. If it has a 115 V / 230 V selector switch, set it to match your mains now — this is the single most expensive thing to get wrong on this page.

Step 1.2 — Land the AC input

With the plug out, connect mains to the supply's input terminals: L (live), N (neutral), and ⏚ (earth). Put a fuse or breaker upstream. Fit the terminal cover and strain relief before you go any further.

Step 1.3 — Build the 24 V bus

You have one supply feeding three consumers (controller, driver, two switches), so make a small distribution point rather than stacking four wires under one screw:

  • Run one wire from the supply's V+ to a 24 V+ distribution point — a small terminal block, a bus bar, or two ferrules doubled into one screw if that is all you have.
  • Do the same from V− to a 24 V− distribution point.
  • Pick a colour convention and keep it for the whole build: this guide uses orange for 24 V+ and blue for 24 V−.

Leave both distribution points otherwise empty for now. Nothing else connects in this stage.

Step 1.4 — Prove the bus, then kill it again

Plug in the AC and switch on, with nothing connected downstream of the bus. Set your meter to DC volts and measure from 24 V+ to 24 V−.

  • You want roughly +24 V (anything ≈ 23.5–25 V is fine; many supplies have a trim pot).
  • A negative reading means your meter leads are swapped, not the supply — swap the leads and confirm which terminal is genuinely positive. Label them.
  • Also check 24 V− to earth and 24 V+ to earth: neither should read a dead short.

Then switch off and pull the AC plug. The rest of the build happens dead.

✔ Verify before Stage 2
  • Meter reads ≈ +24 V from the 24 V+ point to the 24 V− point.
  • Both bus points are clearly labelled + and −, and you are certain which is which.
  • AC terminals are covered; the plug is out; the supply is now dead.

Stage 2The controller

Goal: the FMC4030 powered, earthed, and reachable over Ethernet. Three power wires and one network cable.

The controller is the cheapest stage to wire and the easiest to damage, so it gets its own step. It takes 24 V on VN+/VN− — the same bus the driver uses — plus an earth bond on PE.

24V+ bus ────► VN+ ┐ 24V− bus ────► VN− ├─ FMC4030 power/limit edge earth ⏚ ────► PE ┘ PC ──── RJ45 ────► CN3 (default 192.168.0.30 : 8088)
Step 2.1 — Find the two terminal banks

The FMC4030 has terminals along two opposite edges. Orient yourself before wiring — terminals that happen to sit across from each other are not related:

  • One edge is the signal / output edge: the per-axis 5V, PU, DR groups plus the OP outputs.
  • The other is the power / limit / input edge: VN+, VN−, PE, then the LP/LN/GND limit groups, then IN0–IN3.

In this stage you only touch the first three terminals of the power edge. The full map is in §11.

Step 2.2 — Wire controller power

With the supply dead:

FromTo controllerWire
24 V+ busVN+24 V+
24 V− busVN−24 V−

Double-check these two against your labels before moving on. Reversed polarity on VN+/VN− is the classic way to kill a controller on its first power-up.

Step 2.3 — Bond PE to earth

Run a wire from the controller's PE terminal to the machine frame, and from the frame to protective earth. PE is chassis ground — it is a safety and noise path, not a signal return, so do not use it in place of VN− or a limit GND.

Step 2.4 — Plug in Ethernet and set up the PC

Run an RJ45 cable from CN3 to your PC (directly, or through a switch/router). The controller ships on a fixed address:

  • Controller: IP 192.168.0.30, port 8088.
  • Set your PC's adapter to the same subnet — e.g. IP 192.168.0.35, mask 255.255.255.0, gateway 192.168.0.1.

You will test the link in §9. CN1 (RS-232 DB9) and CN2 (RS-232/485 on RJ45) exist but are not needed for bring-up — leave them alone.

Resist the urge to power up and look at the lights. There is nothing to learn from it yet, and every power-up with half-finished wiring is a chance to short something. The single power-on happens in §9, after the checklist.
✔ Verify before Stage 3
  • VN+ traces back to 24 V+, VN− to 24 V− — confirmed by continuity, not by memory.
  • PE is bonded to the frame and to earth.
  • Ethernet is seated in CN3; the PC adapter is on 192.168.0.x.
  • Still de-energized.

Stage 3The driver

Goal: DIP switches set correctly, 24 V in, and the three signal wires from controller axis 1. Motor still unplugged.

The driver is where most first-time mistakes live, so it splits into three parts: switches first (they are awkward to reach once wired), then power, then signals. Like the controller, the driver has terminals on two ends — a control-signal end (PU±, DR±, MF±, COM24V) and a power/motor end (V+, V−, A+, A−, B+, B−).

3a — Set the DIP switches (do this first)

There are eight switches. SW1–SW3 set the output current, SW4 sets the mode, and SW5–SW8 set microstepping.

SW4 must be OFF. SW4 = OFF selects pulse / subdivision mode, which is what the FMC4030's PU/DR signals require. With SW4 = ON the driver is in I/O mode and will ignore your pulses completely — the motor simply sits there holding, and you will spend an afternoon checking wiring that was fine all along.
Step 3.1 — Set the current to match the motor (2.0 A)

The 57HS motor is rated 2.0 A per phase. Set the driver to that and no more — too low is weak and skips steps, too high runs hot. Peak current is the operating figure × 1.4.

Step 3.2 — Choose microstepping

Microstepping divides each 1.8° full step into smaller ones: smoother and quieter, but more pulses per revolution, so a high setting caps your top speed. 1600 steps/rev is a good general-purpose starting point.

Write the number down. Steps/rev is how the software converts millimetres into pulses. If you change this switch later without telling the software, every distance you command will be wrong by that ratio.

3b — Driver power (the muscle)

Step 3.3 — Feed the driver 24 V
FromTo driverWire
24 V+ busV+24 V+

This is the only power the motor ever sees — all motor current is manufactured here, from this pair of wires. Use conductors sized for a couple of amps and keep them short. The driver accepts DC 20–50 V; 24 V sits comfortably in range.

3c — Signal wiring (the instructions)

Three wires carry everything the controller has to say. They are wired common-anode: the controller's 5 V feeds the driver's two opto-isolator anodes (PU+ and DR+, tied together), and the controller's PU1 and DR1 pins sink the other side of those optos.

FMC4030 — axis 1 FMDD50D40NOM driver ──────────────── ─────────────────── 5V ────────────────┬──────────────► PU+ (opto anode) └──────────────► DR+ (opto anode) PU1 ───────────────────────────────► PU− pulse / step (falling-edge active) DR1 ───────────────────────────────► DR− direction MF+ MF− ── leave OPEN COM24V ── leave OPEN (24 V-signal mode only)
Step 3.4 — Run the three signal wires
Controller (axis 1)DriverWhat it carries
5VPU+ and DR+ (bridge them)5 V supply for both opto-isolator LEDs. Carries ~15–17 mA per input and no motor current at all.
PU1PU−The pulse train. One pulse = one microstep.
DR1DR−Direction: which way the next pulses go.

Note that 5V goes to two terminals — take one wire from the controller to the driver and bridge PU+ to DR+ at the driver end, or land two wires on the controller's 5V. Either is fine.

Use the controller's 5 V here — never 24 V. The PU/DR inputs in this common-anode wiring expect 5 V. Feeding 24 V into them damages the opto inputs. The driver's COM24V terminal exists for a different (24 V-signal) wiring scheme that we are not using — leave it open.
MF+ / MF− stay disconnected. MF is "motor free" — energising it releases the motor so the shaft spins freely with no holding torque. You almost always want holding torque, so leave these two terminals empty. An axis that mysteriously has no torque is usually an MF wire someone added helpfully.
Keep signal and power apart. Run these three signal wires away from the 24 V and motor-coil runs, and keep them reasonably short. Stepper motor wiring is electrically noisy; a pulse line taped alongside it can pick up phantom steps.
✔ Verify before Stage 4
  • SW4 = OFF. Look at it again — it is the most common single-axis failure.
  • Current set to 2.0 A (SW1 OFF, SW2 OFF, SW3 ON); microstepping noted down (1600 suggested).
  • V+/V− polarity correct, verified with continuity back to the labelled bus.
  • Controller 5V reaches both PU+ and DR+; PU1→PU−; DR1→DR−.
  • MF+, MF−, COM24V all empty. Axis 2 and axis 3 terminals on the controller all empty.

Stage 4The rail

Goal: motor coils landed on the driver, and both limit switches reporting back to the controller. Then you are done wiring.

The rail contributes two things electrically: a motor that takes current from the driver, and limit switches that send signals back to the controller. Note they connect to different boxes — a very common point of confusion. The motor never touches the controller; the switches never touch the driver.

4a — Motor → driver

The 57HS motor has four leads on an XH-4 connector: two per coil. The colours map straight across:

PinMotor colourCoilDriver terminal
1BlackA (A+)A+
2GreenĀ (A−)A−
3RedB (B+)B+
4BlueB̄ (B−)B−
Step 4.1 — Confirm the coil pairs with a meter

Before landing anything, prove which wires belong together. With the motor unplugged from everything, measure resistance between pairs:

  • Black ↔ Green should read about 0.75 Ω — they are one coil (A).
  • Red ↔ Blue should read about 0.75 Ω — they are the other coil (B).
  • Any A wire ↔ any B wire should read open. The two coils are electrically separate inside the motor.

If your motor's colours differ from the table, trust the meter and the coil pairs, not the colour chart.

Step 4.2 — Land the four coil wires

With power off, connect Black→A+, Green→A−, Red→B+, Blue→B−.

Pairing matters more than polarity. Getting A+ and A− backwards just reverses the axis direction — annoying, harmless, and fixable. Splitting a pair (say Black and Red on A) means neither coil gets a complete circuit, and the motor will buzz, vibrate in place, or refuse to turn. If the motor misbehaves on first jog, this is the first thing to re-check.
Never connect or disconnect the motor while the driver is powered. Interrupting coil current in a live driver produces a voltage spike that can take out the output stage. Motor in place now, while everything is dead — and power down before you ever touch it again.

4b — Limit / home switches → controller

Each FSK40 is a photoelectric switch with a slot: a flag on the moving carriage passes through the slot and trips it. On the FMC4030 these switches do double duty — end-of-travel protection and the home/origin reference the zero-return routine drives to.

The switches must be NPN, normally-open (NO). The FMC4030's limit inputs are low-level active — the switch works by pulling the signal line down. A PNP switch will never read correctly no matter how you wire it. Verify against the label on your own switch.
FSK40 switch FMC4030 — axis 1 limit group ──────────── ──────────────────────────── Brown ──────► 24V+ bus LP1 ◄── black wire of the positive-end switch Blue ──────► 24V− bus LN1 ◄── black wire of the negative-end switch (home) Black ──────► LP1 or LN1 GND ◄── axis-1 limit ground (sits right after LN1)
Step 4.3 — Mount the sensors and the flag

If this is not already done mechanically: fix each sensor body to the rail so the moving flag passes through the optical gap without touching anything, and fix the flag to the carriage.

  • Put the negative-end sensor at the home/zero end of travel, and the positive-end sensor at the far end.
  • Leave a few millimetres of margin before the hard mechanical stop, so the switch trips before the carriage bottoms out.
  • Slide the carriage by hand through both sensors and watch each sensor's indicator LED change state. Fix any mechanical interference now.
Step 4.4 — Wire both switches

Both switches share the 24 V bus; only their black signal wires differ.

Switch wireNegative-end switch (home)Positive-end switch
Brown (+24 V)24 V+ bus24 V+ bus
Blue (0 V)24 V− bus24 V− bus
Black (signal)Controller LN1Controller LP1

Use the GND terminal that sits in the axis-1 limit group, immediately after LN1, as this pair's limit ground / return. All three limit GND terminals on the controller are internally common, so electrically any of them works — but keeping each pair in its own group keeps the harness readable.

Only fitting one switch? Put it at the home end on LN1. You get homing and protection where the carriage returns, and you then rely on software soft-limits at the far end. Leave LP1 empty.
Wrong end trips? If the software later shows "+" tripping when you move toward the negative end, the two black wires are simply swapped. Power off and exchange them at LP1/LN1.
✔ Verify — wiring complete
  • Coil pairs metered: Black+Green ≈ 0.75 Ω, Red+Blue ≈ 0.75 Ω, A↔B open.
  • Motor landed on A+/A−/B+/B− with pairs intact, done with power off.
  • Both switches: brown→24 V+, blue→24 V−, black→LP1 (far end) / LN1 (home end).
  • Axis-1 GND used as the limit return. LP2/LN2/LP3/LN3 empty.
  • Flag passes cleanly through both sensor slots by hand, LEDs changing state.

8Pre-power checklist

Thirteen connections, still de-energized, meter in hand. Walk the list before the plug goes in.

  • AC landed correctly on the supply's L / N / ⏚; voltage selector correct; terminals covered; fuse or breaker upstream.
  • 24 V bus polarity verified and labelled; no short from either rail to earth.
  • Controller VN+ → 24 V+, VN− → 24 V−. Confirmed, not assumed.
  • Controller PE bonded to frame and protective earth.
  • Driver V+ → 24 V+, V− → 24 V−.
  • Driver SW4 = OFF (pulse mode). Current 2.0 A: SW1 OFF, SW2 OFF, SW3 ON. Microstepping set and written down.
  • Signals: 5V → PU+ & DR+; PU1 → PU−; DR1 → DR−.
  • MF+, MF−, COM24V left open.
  • Motor coil pairs verified by continuity; four coil wires on A+/A−/B+/B−; connector seated while unpowered.
  • Limit switches are NPN-NO; brown/blue on the 24 V bus; black wires on the correct LP1/LN1.
  • Nothing landed on axis 2 or axis 3 terminals.
  • Ethernet in CN3; PC adapter on 192.168.0.x / 255.255.255.0.
  • No stray strands, no terminal touching its neighbour, every screw tight, every wire tug-tested.
  • Carriage sitting mid-travel (not against a hard stop), and the path is clear of hands and tools.

9First power-on & first jog

  1. Energize. Plug in and switch on. The supply LED should light, the controller and driver power LEDs should come on, and the driver's red ALM fault light should stay off. If ALM is lit, power down immediately and go to §10.
  2. Listen and feel. The motor should be silently holding — try turning the shaft or coupling by hand; it should resist noticeably. No resistance means either no 24 V at the driver or an accidentally-energised MF.
  3. Test the network. From the PC: ping 192.168.0.30 -t. You want steady replies. No replies means the PC is not on the controller's subnet, or the cable is not in CN3.
  4. Connect the software. Launch the controller software and connect to 192.168.0.30 : 8088. If the connection drops after about a minute of inactivity, that is the controller's idle timeout by design — just reconnect.
  5. Check the limit switches before commanding any motion. In the status panel, slide the carriage by hand and trip each switch. Confirm the home-end switch registers as LN1 (negative) and the far-end switch as LP1 (positive). If they are reversed, power down and swap the two black wires. Fix this now — a limit wired to the wrong end cannot stop an overrun.
  6. First jog: a few millimetres, low speed. Command the smallest move your software allows on axis 1. Watch for:
    • Smooth rotation and travel → coils are paired correctly.
    • Buzzing, vibration in place, or no rotation → power down and re-check the coil pairing (§4a).
    • Nothing at all, motor still holding → almost certainly SW4 is ON, or PU/DR is mis-wired.
  7. Check the direction convention. Jog positive and confirm the carriage moves toward the positive-end switch. If it goes the wrong way, either invert the direction in software, or power down and swap the motor's A+ ↔ A−. For a single axis, software is the easier fix.
  8. Confirm the limits actually stop motion. At low speed, jog toward each end and confirm the axis stops when the switch trips. Stay ready to cut power.
  9. Home the axis. Run the zero-return / homing routine to LN1 and set that position as zero. Your axis now has a repeatable origin, which is what makes every coordinate you command afterwards meaningful.
Moving on to software. In the SDK this axis is index 0 (the terminals labelled PU1/DR1). The controller counts axes from zero while the silkscreen counts from one — an off-by-one worth remembering. The software also needs the steps/rev you set in Step 3.2 to convert millimetres into pulses.
Single axis complete. Smooth jogging in both directions, both limits verified, and a repeatable home position means your electrical build is sound. Adding a second axis is the same four stages again on 5V/PU3/DR3 with limits on LP3/LN3 — the bus and the controller are already there.
